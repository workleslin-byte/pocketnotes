Notebooks of Ancient Singers

Before paper, the entire Iliad lived inside the memory of singers who could not read. When writing arrived, it did not replace that memory. It extended it — outward, onto surfaces that could outlast the body holding the thought.

A blind old man, standing in a hall somewhere near the Aegean coast, is performing a poem 15,693 lines long. He has not read it. He cannot read. He is reconstructing it in real time from thousands of memorised formulas, stock scenes, and structural patterns accumulated over a lifetime of performance. The words shift slightly each time. The poem stays the same.

We know this is how the Iliad survived because two American classicists proved it in the field rather than in the library. In 1928, Milman Parry submitted his doctoral thesis at the Sorbonne arguing that the Homeric epics were composed orally — that phrases like rhododaktylos eos (“rosy-fingered dawn”) and oinopa ponton (“the wine-dark sea”) were not poetic decoration but mnemonic units, sized to fit Greek hexameter, deployed by bards the way an improvisational musician deploys scales. To test the theory, Parry and his student Albert Lord travelled to Yugoslavia between 1933 and 1935 to record the guslari — the South Slavic singers who still composed epic poems orally in the early twentieth century. Their finest informant, Avdo Međedović, performed a song called The Wedding of Smailagic Meho over five days. It ran to 13,000 lines. Parry wrote that listening to Međedović perform, “one has the overwhelming sense that, in some way, one is hearing Homer.”

What Parry and Lord established — published later as Lord’s The Singer of Tales (1960) — is that the oral tradition was a complete information technology with its own architecture for storing, retrieving, and transmitting knowledge across generations. The singers were not reciting from memory the way an actor learns lines. They were composing in performance, fluently, using a shared inheritance of formulas that let them construct epics of any length on the spot.

The poem existed in the bard. The bard existed because the poem could not exist anywhere else.

When writing arrived, it did not kill this. It changed where the architecture lived.

The Greeks had a word for the tools that aided memory both before and after writing came: hypomnemata. The term translates loosely as “memoranda” or “reminders” — the marks, notes, and jottings made to hold an idea in place when internal recall was insufficient. Aristotle used the word. Plato used it too, with deep ambivalence. In the Phaedrus, written around 370 BCE, Plato has Socrates tell the myth of Theuth, the Egyptian god who invented writing and presented it to King Thamus. Thamus refused the gift. “This invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it,” he said, “because they will not practice their memory.” Writing, Thamus warned, would give people the appearance of wisdom without the substance.

Plato was wrong about the direction of the effect. People who write do not become stupider. He was not entirely wrong about the cost, though. Writing does change what we bother to remember and how we hold it. What it gives in return is something different in kind from oral memory: an external architecture. Ideas that do not have to live inside a single skull. Ideas that can outlast their author, be read by strangers, accumulate annotation across centuries.

Michel Foucault, in a 1983 essay titled “Self Writing”, returned to the hypomnemata and made a distinction that most modern thinking about notebooks has missed. The hypomnemata, Foucault argued, were not confessional diaries. They were not about uncovering hidden truths about the self. They were “books of life” — collections of quotations, observations, arguments worth keeping — whose purpose was, in his exact phrase, “the shaping of the self.” The notes were not the record of who someone was. They were the material from which a self was built. Foucault quoted Seneca approvingly: the writer should equip the mind “with many arts, many precepts, and patterns of conduct taken from many epochs of history; but all should blend harmoniously into one.” The notebook, in this conception, is a tool for becoming someone, not a record of someone you already are.

✦   ✦   ✦

This is older than Greece. The Mesopotamians were keeping notes by 3200 BCE — not poetry but grain inventories, debt records, legal contracts, lists of names. The bureaucratic mind preceded the literary one by a thousand years. The clay tablets that tracked barley shipments and tax payments were, through sheer accumulation, the first external memory of a civilisation.

Then something interesting happened. The same medium that held the invoice began holding the elegy. By 1800 BCE, scribes were writing down older oral material — songs, hymns, prayers, stories that had circulated by mouth for generations. Around 1200 BCE, the Standard Babylonian Version of the Epic of Gilgamesh was inscribed on twelve clay tablets at Nineveh: a poem about a king and his companion and the grief of watching someone you love die. It is the oldest written story we have. The library at Nineveh, where the tablets were excavated in the 1850s, held administrative records on the same shelves — lists of officials, building accounts, astronomical observations. The surface that held the receipt held the elegy. The Assyrian librarians did not consider this strange.

What the ancient world understood, in practice if not always in theory, is that notation extends cognition. You cannot hold everything inside your head. You can hold a great deal in your head and your notes together, as a combined system — the way a musician keeps some things in the fingers and some in the sheet music, and the performance lives in the seam between them.

✦   ✦   ✦

This is what a pocket notebook is doing, in its unremarkable modern way. It is not replacing your thinking. It is extending it outward, the way the bards extended their memory into formulas and the way the Mesopotamians extended their accounting into clay. The medium changes across three thousand years. The impulse holds: I have thought something. I do not want to lose it. Let me make a mark.

Three thousand years from now, no one will excavate a pocket notebook the way archaeologists excavated the tablets at Nineveh. The paper will not survive. The ink will not survive. The thought you wrote down this morning, though — the half-formed line, the question you needed to remember, the sentence that surprised you — is at least as likely to be the one you build something from, and the act of writing it down connects you to something considerably older than the app on your phone.

The notebook is not a new technology. It is one of the oldest we have. It happens to fit in a pocket now, but it has been the same tool since the first scribe pressed a stylus into wet clay in a Sumerian street market and wrote down a number he could not afford to forget.

The mark outside the mind. That is the technology.

Everything else is a variation.
