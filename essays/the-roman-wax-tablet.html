The Roman Wax Tablet

Cicero drafted his speeches on it. Pliny the Elder filled a hundred and sixty of them. Two thousand years before the pocket notebook, Rome had already solved the problem of where to put a thought the moment you have it.

Somewhere in the second century BCE, a Roman crossing the Forum carried, tucked into the folds of his toga, a pair of thin wooden leaves hinged at the spine. The inner faces were hollowed out and filled with beeswax, darkened with soot. A stylus hung from the frame on a cord — pointed at one end for writing, flattened at the other for rubbing out. The whole object was about the size of the notebook in your pocket.

This was the tabula cerata, and it was everywhere in the Roman world. Children learned their letters on it. Merchants kept accounts, judges took notes, lovers sent word across town, and Cicero scratched out the bones of an oration before he rose to deliver it. The wax cost almost nothing. The stylus was quick. When the words had done their work, the flat end pressed them back into a smooth grey blank, and the tablet was ready again. Its whole virtue was that it forgot.

The teacher Quintilian, setting down his manual on rhetoric around 95 CE, was exact about why wax and nothing finer.

“It is best to write on wax, owing to the facility which it offers for erasure.”

— Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria, X.3.31

He pressed the point. Parchment, he warned, slows the hand and breaks the train of thought, because the pen keeps having to stop and take more ink. The Romans had named friction as the enemy of composition nineteen centuries ago. What they wanted from a surface was that it vanish under the hand.

Pliny the Elder carried the habit to its limit. His nephew left a portrait of how the man worked: a secretary beside him through every waking hour, reading aloud while he dictated notes — on the road in a litter, at dinner, in the bath, his hands wrapped against the winter so the cold could not cost him an hour. He thought walking was time stolen from study. He left a hundred and sixty notebooks behind, written small on both sides, and from that dense mass the Naturalis Historia was assembled. He died at Vesuvius in 79 CE, close enough to the eruption to take notes.

The tablet was always the tool of first resort, because papyrus was the opposite of immediate. A sheet had to be bought, prepared, and covered in ink that dried slowly and lifted badly. So the Romans split the act of writing in two. Wax took the thought while it was still moving. Papyrus took the thought once it had earned its keep. You did not commit anything to papyrus, any more than you would now write a finished essay straight onto the first blank page.

We know the texture of all this because the tablets keep coming back up. Between 2010 and 2014, archaeologists from the Museum of London pulled 405 Roman stylus tablets from the wet black mud of the Walbrook, where the lack of oxygen had held the wood intact for nineteen centuries. The scholar Roger Tomlin spent years reading the marks the styluses had cut into the grain beneath the wax. The earliest carries a date you can still read: the eighth of January, AD 57. What lasted two thousand years under the City of London was not a poem, or a law, or a line of history. It was a note about a debt of 105 denarii.

✦   ✦   ✦

That is the entire case for the pocket notebook, made in advance by people who would never see one. The tablet was the place you were allowed to be wrong — it took the half-formed guess, the figure you needed for an hour and then never again, the sentence you would throw out by morning. You write to think, and whatever survives is a by-product. The notebook holds that same place in the day, except the page stays instead of being smoothed flat.

Caesar’s officers carried the same tablets into the field, where the surface set its own rule: wax holds only so much before the marks run together and blur. An order had to compress to the essential — what must happen, in what sequence, and who answers for it. The decision, cut small and hard into the wax.

Two thousand years stand between Pliny in his litter and someone writing on the train to work. The materials changed. The problem did not. I have a thought. I need to hold it before it goes. What do I write it on?

The wax tablet answered that for a thousand years. The pocket notebook has been answering it for three hundred. The answer holds because the question never moved.
