We Don’t Compete With Notebooks

The real competition is the back of a receipt, the notes app you never open again, and the thing you were going to remember but didn’t.

The thought arrives in the queue at the bank. Or in the back of an auto with the engine too loud to form a complete sentence. Or in the thirty seconds of dead air between parking and walking into the meeting. The expensive notebook is at home on the desk. What is available is a receipt, a palm, or a screenshot of nothing.

Most people who buy a beautiful notebook do not use it. They buy it because it looks like the kind of object a person who writes would own. Then it sits on the desk, admired, while the phone opens instead. The notebook travels to a new city in a bag. It comes back. It goes on the shelf next to the others.

The cause is the notebook’s own condition. A notebook held in reserve, waiting for thoughts worthy of it, quietly cancels the thoughts that arrive.

✦   ✦   ✦

The problem is rarely motivation. People who want to write things down want to write things down. The problem is availability. The idea arrives in the queue, on the walk between meetings, mid-conversation in a room you cannot pause. In those moments, the phone becomes the default capture surface because it is the only surface present.

But using a phone to hold a fleeting thought is like using a kitchen tap to fill a thimble — the infrastructure is completely out of proportion to the task. Unlock, find the app, tap, type with a thumb, fight the autocorrect, lock. By the third step the thought has shifted shape. By the fifth, it has been replaced by the notification that arrived during step four.

Anthony Trollope wrote forty-seven novels while working full-time as a surveyor’s clerk for the British Post Office. In his posthumously published An Autobiography (1883, Chapter VII), he left the governing principle: “A small daily task, if it be really daily, will beat the labours of a spasmodic Hercules. It is the tortoise which always catches the hare.” What is less often noted is where he actually wrote: on trains. His Post Office work sent him around Ireland on long journeys, and he used the transit time — noisy, moving, shared — to produce his pages. The compartment was not ideal. It was there. Availability is not a minor advantage. It is the condition that makes all other advantages possible.

“A small daily task, if it be really daily, will beat the labours of a spasmodic Hercules.”

— Anthony Trollope, An Autobiography, 1883
✦   ✦   ✦

The actual competition is not other notebooks. It is the sticky note on the monitor that no one re-reads. The screenshot taken instead of writing. The voice memo that will never be transcribed. The napkin, the receipt, the back of the hand. These are what people reach for when a notebook is not present — or when the notebook present feels too important to spend on a half-formed thought.

An A6 notebook rides on the body, not in the bag that might be zipped shut when the idea arrives. It fits in a jacket pocket and the back pocket of jeans. When the moment comes, the notebook is already there. Open, write, close. Seven seconds.

The Founders’ 500 is not a ceremonial object. No embossed leather, no clasp, no air of waiting for the thought that deserves it. It is a working surface. A notebook that feels too good to use does not get used. A notebook that feels like a tool gets used the way tools get used: constantly, without ceremony, usually while standing up.

✦   ✦   ✦

The grid helps here. It does not suggest that what goes on it should be neat or complete or worthy of permanent record. It is a neutral surface, nothing more. Diagrams go on it. Quick lists. The word you cannot remember how to spell. The thing due before five. The idea that arrived mid-sentence in a conversation you could not pause.

All of it goes in, without preamble, without organisation. What it means can be decided later. What matters first is that it was caught at all.

The competition is every moment when nothing is there to receive the thought. The gap between the idea and the page — that is what we are solving for.
