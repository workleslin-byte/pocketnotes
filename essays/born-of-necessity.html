Born of Necessity

The best tools disappear into use. The notebook that fits in your pocket was never designed to impress anyone. It was designed to always be there.

A carpenter's best chisel is not the newest one in the set. It is the one that fits the hand, that has been sharpened so many times the handle is dark with oil, that gets reached for without thinking. It works, reliably, every time, and has never once gotten in the way of the work it is there to do. The beauty, if it is there at all, arrived the same way the grip did — through use.

Tools earned that way have a quality that designed things rarely achieve. They are not optimised for admiration. The optimisation happened in the field, not the studio, and the field is a more honest editor than any designer.

In 1896, the American architect Louis Sullivan published an essay called “The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered” in Lippincott’s Magazine. He was arguing against architects who dressed new building types in old ornamentation — who built steel-frame skyscrapers and covered them in Greco-Roman columns the structure no longer needed. His answer was a sentence that has governed good design ever since: “Whether it be the sweeping eagle in his flight or the open apple blossom, form ever follows function, and this is the law.”

Sullivan was not arguing for austerity. His own buildings were dense with ornament — the Wainwright Building in St. Louis, the Guaranty Building in Buffalo, both encrusted with terracotta detail. He was arguing that the form of a thing should emerge from what the thing must do, not from what the designer wishes the thing to express. Function was not the enemy of form. It was the source of it, and anything that forgot this would eventually look like exactly what it was: an answer to a question nobody asked.

The pocket notebook has this quality. Not because it was invented with particular genius, but because it survived. Hundreds of formats came and went — folios, wax tablets, elaborate bound journals with clasps and lock mechanisms, personal organisers with plastic inserts and tab dividers, ring binders that bent pages on every turn. The pocket notebook remains because it solved the actual problem: you need to write something down, and you are almost certainly not at a desk.

A6 — 105 by 148 millimetres — fits in the breast pocket of a jacket without creating a silhouette. It fits in the back pocket of jeans without bending. It sits in one hand without requiring two to hold it open. These are not aesthetic choices. They are engineering conclusions reached by generations of people who needed something to write on while standing, walking, sitting in the back of a car, waiting in a corridor. The form was determined by the function being performed under those conditions. Sullivan’s law, pressed flat and put in a pocket.

The grid holds without directing.

What gets added matters as much as what is left out. A grid serves more tasks than any alternative — text, diagrams, quick tables, rough proportions, margin alignment. The grid is neutral enough to support any kind of thinking without imposing a format. A blank page asks the writer to invent structure from nothing. A lined page imposes horizontal thinking on problems that do not move horizontally. The grid holds without directing.

The cover is thick because it is used as a writing surface. You are frequently writing without a table. The cover must be rigid enough to press against without putting the notebook down first. This is a decision reached by anyone who has ever tried to write against a floppy cover while standing on a train platform with one hand holding a bag.

The binding is sewn rather than glued because glued bindings fail. Not immediately — they hold for months. At some point, usually when you most need to write something quickly, a page detaches. A sewn binding does not degrade this way. The book holds together for as long as you use it, and then holds together on the shelf. Longevity through construction, not through care.

✦   ✦   ✦

None of this is precious. It is the correct answer to a specific question: what does a notebook need to be so that it is always with you and always ready?

Small. Durable. Opens flat so the spine does not fight the hand. Pages thick enough that ink does not bleed through to the facing side. Cover stiff enough to write against. A format that fits without announcing itself.

These are necessity requirements. They were not invented in a studio. They were discovered by people who needed to write things down and kept running into the same problems, and kept modifying the format until the problems stopped. The notebook that survived all of that is not the most beautiful one, or the most expensive, or the most elaborately designed.

It is the one that got out of the way. Sullivan called that a law. He was right about skyscrapers. He was right about this too.

References

  • Sullivan, L.H. (1896). “The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered.” Lippincott’s Magazine, March 1896.
