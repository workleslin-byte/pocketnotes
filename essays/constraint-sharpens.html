Constraint
Sharpens

An A6 page holds about two hundred words. Deciding which two hundred is the entire discipline.

Before The Waste Land was the poem everyone knows, it was a sprawling manuscript that T.S. Eliot handed to Ezra Pound. Pound cut it nearly in half. He struck whole passages, crossed out openings Eliot was proud of, and handed back something leaner and stranger. Eliot kept almost every cut. He dedicated the published poem to Pound with a phrase borrowed from Dante — il miglior fabbro, the better craftsman. What we read is what survived the deletion.

Most writing never meets a Pound. The sprawling draft stays sprawling, because the person holding the pen is the worst-placed person to cut it. This is the quiet work a small page does. An A6 sheet is 105 by 148 millimetres, and at a normal hand it holds around two hundred words. The limit arrives early, before you have talked yourself into the long version, and it asks the one question that matters: which two hundred?

This is the part that feels like deprivation and turns out to be clarification. A page with room for everything lets you postpone the decision about what the thing actually is. A page with room for two hundred words makes you decide now, in the first minute, while the idea is still warm. You write the claim instead of the throat-clearing in front of the claim. You find out whether you have a thought or only a mood.

Antoine de Saint-Exupéry spent his working life as a pilot, and his most quoted sentence is about aircraft rather than prose. “It seems that perfection is attained,” he wrote in Terre des hommes in 1939, “not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away.” A wing gets faster as weight comes off it. A sentence works the same way. The long version is the one you wrote. The short version is the one you meant.

The long version is the one you wrote. The short version is the one you meant.

✦   ✦   ✦

John McPhee has written for The New Yorker for half a century, and in Draft No. 4 he describes a habit he picked up earlier at Time, called greening. An editor would return a finished piece with a note in the margin — Green 5, or Green 9 — meaning cut that many lines to fit the column, and change nothing about the voice or the sense. McPhee found the forced cut almost always improved the piece. The constraint did what his own judgement, left to itself, would not.

In 1960, Bennett Cerf, who ran Random House, bet Theodor Geisel fifty dollars that he could not write a book using fifty distinct words or fewer. Writing as Dr. Seuss, Geisel came back with Green Eggs and Ham — forty-nine words of one syllable and a single longer one, anywhere. It has since sold more than two hundred million copies. Fifty words were enough to write a book that children memorise before they can read it.

✦   ✦   ✦

A notebook page that runs out before the thought runs out of breath is greening you in advance. The next page is always there, but turning it is a small commitment, a quiet question: is this worth continuing? Often the answer is no, and the cut happens before the words are spent. The page made the decision you would have talked yourself out of. That is the page doing its job.

William Strunk told his students to omit needless words. A small page omits them for you, by refusing to hold them. What is left when the room runs out is usually the idea itself, without the scaffolding it arrived in. Two lines that carry something outlast two pages that circle it.

Write on a smaller page. Watch what you stop saying.
