How to Keep Yourself

A note-taking system for people who almost never take notes. The point is not productivity, and it is not journaling. The point is to stop being a stranger to the person you were a year ago — and here is exactly what it costs, what the research says it does to you, and who you become by day ninety.

You are not a note-taker. Your phone holds two or three note apps you opened once, a camera roll thick with screenshots you will never look at again, and somewhere in a drawer there is a good notebook someone gave you, three pages used and the rest blank. This is not a character flaw. It is the default. Things move you a few times a week — a sentence in a book, an idea on a walk, a small realisation about someone you love — and every one of them passes through and leaves nothing behind.

Call this the sparse note-taker, and assume it describes you, because it describes almost everyone. The sparse note-taker is not short of thoughts. The sparse note-taker simply has nowhere for a thought to go, so it goes nowhere. You are not shallow. You are untraced. The difference between those two words is the entire subject of this guide.

The cost is quiet, and it compounds in the dark. Try to say what you believed three years ago and you will produce a vague shape, sanded smooth by retelling. Try to name what you were reading, what you were afraid of, what changed your mind and on what day — and there is nothing to check it against. The years fuse into one long present tense. You have had a life, and kept almost no evidence that you were the one who lived it.

What a notebook is actually for

Start with an idea that sounds abstract and turns out to be the most practical thing in here: a self is not something you have. It is something you can read back. You feel continuous — the person waking up is obviously the one who fell asleep — but the feeling is the whole of it. Of the actual contents of your mind twelve months ago you have kept nothing you could put in front of a jury. The continuity is a hunch. A notebook is where the hunch becomes evidence.

Memory will not do this job, because memory is not a recording. It is a story you retell in the present, quietly rewritten each time, more flattering to you than the facts wherever it can manage. The version of last year that lives in your head is a rumour. A dated page is a witness. It keeps what you genuinely thought, in the words you genuinely used, including the opinions you have since disowned and would rather not be shown. That last part is the value no one mentions. A notebook is the one region of your past that cannot be re-spun to suit who you are today.

Joan Didion said it better than anyone in a 1968 essay, On Keeping a Notebook. “I think we are well advised to keep on nodding terms with the people we used to be,” she wrote, “whether we find them attractive company or not.” The record of what happened was never her reason. Her reason is the sentence to carry out of here: “Remember what it was to be me: that is always the point.”

None of this will make you more productive, and it is not therapy. The promise is smaller and stranger than either of those, and better than both. Keep this up for three months and you will own something almost nobody has — a way to stand outside your own mind and see that it has been moving the entire time, in directions you never noticed while you were in there living them.

“But I have nothing worth writing down”

One objection stops most people before the first mark on the first page, and it needs answering head-on, because you are almost certainly having it right now. It goes: notebooks are for people with something to say. Thinkers. Writers. People whose thoughts are quotable. My days are ordinary and my thoughts are not, and I would open the book and sit there with nothing to put in it.

That objection runs the whole process backwards. You do not keep a notebook because you already own thoughts worth keeping. You keep one, and the thoughts turn out to have been there the entire time, folded inside things you barely registered noticing. A self is not delivered whole, to be copied down. It accumulates, out of a few hundred small snags that caught you for reasons you could not have explained on the day — and the pattern those snags make, read back months later, is more truly you than anything you could have sat down and announced.

Didion is useful here too. The real contents of a notebook, she wrote, are “bits of the mind’s string too short to use, an indiscriminate and erratic assemblage with meaning only for its maker.” There is your permission, in one line. You are not composing. You are keeping string. Each scrap looks like nothing on the day you catch it. Its meaning does not live in the scrap; it lives in the pile, and the pile takes months to gather.

So delete the word “worth.” The test for whether a thing goes in the book is not whether it is profound, finished, or defensible in an argument. The test is whether it caught you. A snag, not a verdict. A sentence that made you slow down. An idea that gave you a small private jolt. A thing that irritated you and you cannot say why. That is the entire entry requirement. Catch it now. You can work out later what it was.

You do not keep a notebook because you already have thoughts worth keeping. You keep one, and the thoughts turn out to have been there all along.

One book, one loop

Here is the system, and it is one system, not a bag of tips. A single notebook doing three things that feed one another. It catches your own thoughts. It harvests the lines that move you in what you read and watch and overhear. And on a slow, forgiving schedule, it lets you look back over both. Three motions — capture, harvest, reflect — but they are not three chores to keep alive in parallel. They are a single loop, and the loop is the entire point.

They share the same book and the same dated pages deliberately. An idea you had on Tuesday sits directly beneath a line you copied from a novel, which sits directly above a sentence about the argument you had at work. On the day you write them, the three have nothing to do with one another. Read them together six months on and they turn out to rhyme. That accidental neighbouring — the private idea next to the borrowed quote next to the confession — is the moment a notebook stops being storage and starts being a mind. Split the three across separate apps and separate books and you destroy the one property that made the whole exercise worth the effort.

You will not build all of this on the first day, and you must not try. You start with one notebook, and everything goes into it, undivided, in the order it arrives. The structure comes later, and it comes by itself — the way a path appears across a field once enough feet have crossed the same line. For now there is one book, and one rule: it holds whatever caught you, in the order you caught it.

The method

Carry one small object. Buy a notebook small enough to be on you at every hour, and a pen that lives clipped to it. Pocket size, because a notebook left at home is one you consult after the moment has already closed, which is the same as never. The whole system rests on a single unglamorous fact: the book has to be present when the thing happens. A thought does not wait for you to reach a desk. It arrives on a walk, in a queue, three words into someone else’s sentence, and it has a half-life of about a minute. Whatever is in your pocket wins. Whatever is across the room is lost. Pick the object that will genuinely be on you, and carry it the way you carry your keys.

Ruin page one on purpose. The blank first page will try to make you wait until you have something good enough to earn it. Refuse it flatly. Open the book, write today’s date, and set down the first ordinary thing in reach — what you are drinking, the seat you are in, a flat sentence about the weather that you do not even mean. The page is used now. Nothing on it is precious, because the first mark was chosen precisely for not being. Every notebook that dies blank died because its owner was still waiting to become worthy of page one. You are never going to feel worthy of it. You are going to write on it anyway, today, and that settles it.

Capture: catch your own thoughts in ten seconds. Capture is the reflex the rest is built on, and it costs about ten seconds. When a thought lands — an observation, a question, a disagreement, a half-plan, a thing you noticed about a person — you write it in as few words as will carry it, and you stop. You do not develop it. You do not grow it into a paragraph. You catch it and get back to your day. Date it, because a thought without a date is one you can never later fit into the story of yourself. The discipline is entirely in the not-elaborating. Five words caught will always beat the five hundred you meant to write tonight and never did.

Harvest: keep the lines that move you. Half of what is worth keeping is not yours. It comes from what you read and watch and overhear, and most of it is gone within the hour. Harvesting is the repair, and it is the oldest note habit there is: for centuries readers kept a commonplace book, copying the passages that struck them into a book of their own, so a lifetime of reading left a residue instead of a blank shelf in the memory. You are going to do the same thing, minus the ceremony. Read with the pen within reach. When a line snags — in a novel, an article, a song, a line of dialogue, a thing a friend says that you could not put better yourself — copy it. The actual words, not your gist of them, with a note of where it came from.

The copying matters far more than it looks. A screenshot files the words in a place you will not return to; writing them by hand runs them through you once, slowly, at the pace of your own arm, and that slow pass is what makes them hold. There is a reason it holds. In 1977, T. B. Rogers and colleagues showed that we remember material we process in relation to ourselves better than material we process any other way — the self-reference effect, deeper even than working out what a word means. A line you choose to copy, because it caught something in you, is self-reference in its purest form. You are also, without meaning to, drawing a self-portrait. Ten people read the same book and underline ten different sets of sentences. The set you underline is a fact about you. Gather enough of them and you are looking at the exact shape of your own attention, which is about as near to a fingerprint as the inner life ever comes.

What a week actually looks like. None of this is as effortful as it sounds, and a real week is mostly small. A page from an ordinary one might read: Monday, the way she said “fine” in the meeting and meant the exact opposite — nobody else caught it. Monday again, a line copied from the novel on the train. Tuesday, the onboarding doc should be a two-minute video; nobody reads the PDF and everybody pretends they did. Wednesday, why do I dread Sunday evenings now when I never did at twenty-five? Thursday, a sentence from a show that landed harder than the show deserved. Friday, fixed the bike myself for the first time and felt a completely disproportionate amount of joy about it. Six lines, thirty seconds each, across a week you would otherwise have thrown away whole.

Date everything, and keep an index. By now you have a book filling with your thoughts and other people’s lines, in the order they arrived, which is to say in no order at all. Left there, it is a shoebox. Two small habits convert the shoebox into a system. The first you already have — date every entry. The second is an index. Leave the first two or three pages blank when you begin, and as the book fills, list on them the entries you might want to find again: a few words and a page number. “The thing about my father, p.14.” “Quote on courage, Baldwin, p.31.” “Idea for the side project, p.40.” The index is the whole difference between a pile you never reopen and a book you can think with — because a thought you can find is one you can build on, and a thought you cannot find might as well have evaporated on the walk home.

Return once a week. Once a week, fifteen minutes, you go back and reread the last seven days. Everyone skips this, and it is the step that does the real work — capture and harvest only fill the book; the return is what turns the filling into something you own. Three things happen when you reread the week. You spot the entries that turned out to matter and add them to the index. You catch, now and then, that Tuesday’s idea and Thursday’s quote are the same thought in two coats. And you write two lines about the week itself — not what you did, but what it was like to be you inside it — which will be the most valuable thing in the entire book when you read it a year from now. The return is not busywork. In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed that pulling a memory back out fixes it far more firmly than reading it again — the testing effect. Your weekly reread is that test, run on your own life.

Let it grow into a system. Around the second month, the single notebook starts telling you what it wants to become. The quotes begin to feel as though they want to live together, so you give them the back pages and let them run forward while the daily capture runs from the front. A project you keep circling wants its own stretch of pages, so you give it a thread and note it in the index. The two-line weekly reflections start to feel like the truest thing in the book, and one day you will want a separate home for them — the book of who you are becoming. You plan none of this. You let the use carve the structure, because a system you grow into fits you, and a system you are handed almost never does.

The next ninety days

You will feel none of this on day one, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What follows is the arc if you simply keep the reflex alive — imperfectly, missing days, doing it badly — for three months. It is worth knowing the shape in advance, because the early part is the part that makes most people quit, and it is quietly the most important.

By day 10, it feels awkward and slightly pointless. You forget the book half the time, and the entries you do make look trivial on the page — the string too short to use. You have maybe a dozen of them. Then something small happens: you flip back to the first page and the person who wrote it is already faintly a stranger. That flicker — I’d half-forgotten I thought that — is the entire system arriving in miniature. It is also the first sign that the pen has begun, very slightly, to change what you notice during the day, because you are now walking around with somewhere to put things.

By day 30, the reflex is appearing. You have started reaching for the book without deciding to, mid-sentence, before the thought can escape. The habit is not automatic yet — the research is blunt about the timeline, and thirty days is early — but the resistance is thinning. You have done the weekly return three or four times, so the book is no longer just accumulating; it is beginning to talk to itself. The first real cross-connection lands: two entries a fortnight apart turn out to be the same idea, and seeing them touch tells you something you did not know you thought. You read differently now. You read with a pen.

By day 60, you are near the place the science points to. In 2010, Phillippa Lally and her colleagues at University College London tracked people forming a daily habit and found it took an average of 66 days to turn automatic — with a range from 18 days to well over 200, and, crucially, missing a single day did nothing to the outcome. Somewhere in here you cross that line. Not writing the thing down starts to feel like the effortful option, the small itch of a thought left un-caught. The book has a working index, a couple of months of dated pages, a growing back-section of harvested lines. Because you have been rereading, not just filling, the material has set — those returns were retrieval practice, and it is why so much of it has stayed. You reread a page from a month ago and catch yourself in the act of having changed your mind. That is new. That has never been visible to you before.

By day 90, you are not a sparse note-taker anymore, and the change is structural rather than motivational. The one book has begun to divide itself — daily capture from the front, quotes from the back, a thread or two for the things you keep returning to, and the weekly reflections pulling quietly toward a book of their own. You have an analog footprint now, a physical record of a mind at work, indexed and datable and honest. You notice more, because keeping trains the eye to read the world for what is worth taking out of it. You lose fewer ideas, because they finally have a floor to land on. And you can do the thing you set out to do without knowing you had: pick up the earliest pages and meet the person who wrote them. There is the worry that came to nothing. There is the idea you forgot you had, better than the one you are working on now. There is a whole version of you, in your own hand, on a Tuesday you would otherwise have lost without a trace.

None of the ninety days asks for much. A line here, a copied sentence there, a quarter hour on a Sunday. It survives a real life precisely because it never demands one — and because, as Lally found, a missed day is not a broken streak but a normal Tuesday. This is a reflex you are growing, not a chain you are protecting.

Begin

Here is the trick the whole system plays on you. You set out to keep your thoughts, and you end up keeping yourself — not the tidy self you would hand to a room, but the real, dated, contradictory one that the tidy self is only a press release for. You did not find that self and then write it down. You wrote things down, and the self was in the writing the entire time, waiting the way a photograph waits in the tray, slowly, until it is suddenly there.

Didion, one last time, plainly: how it felt to me, that is getting closer to the truth about a notebook. Not the record of what happened. The record of what it was like to be the one it happened to.

You do not have to believe a word of this to start, and you do not have to be sure it will work. You need a small notebook, a pen that lives with it, and one line on the first page. Put today’s date. Write down the first thing that caught you today, whatever it was, however small.

You have already started to keep yourself.
